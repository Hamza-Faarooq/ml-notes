Chapter 51

K-Means Clustering (The Benchmark)

The Intuition: You are at a massive party and want to group people into \(K\) distinct friend groups based on their physical locations in the room. You drop \(K\) random anchors (centroids) into the room. People walk to their nearest anchor. The anchors then move to the exact center of the people surrounding them. Repeat until no one moves.

The Mathematics & Geometry

K-Means strictly tries to minimize the Within-Cluster Sum of Squares (WCSS). It wants to make the clusters as physically tight as possible.

WCSS Objective Function \[ \arg\min_S \sum_{i=1}^k \sum_{x \in S_i} ||x - \mu_i||^2 \]
  • The E-Step (Expectation): Assign every point \(x\) to the nearest centroid \(\mu_i\) using Euclidean distance. This physically partitions the space into a Voronoi Tessellation (rigid geometric polygons).
  • The M-Step (Maximization): Recalculate the centroid \(\mu_i\) as the mathematical mean of all points assigned to it.

🎯 The CDC Interview Traps

1. "Why does K-means sometimes give terrible results?"
Your Answer: K-Means is highly sensitive to initialization. If you drop two centroids right next to each other, they get trapped in a local minimum. This is solved by K-Means++, which explicitly forces initial centroids to be initialized as far away from each other as physically possible.

2. "Does K-means work on all data shapes?"
Your Answer: No. Because it relies on Euclidean distance from a central mean, it implicitly assumes all clusters are perfectly spherical and roughly the same size. If your data is shaped like two interlocking crescent moons, K-means will violently slice them in half.

Chapter 52

Hierarchical Clustering (The Family Tree)

The Intuition: Instead of guessing \(K\) upfront, what if we build a complete family tree of the data? We start by assuming every single data point is its own isolated cluster. Then, we find the two closest clusters and merge them. We repeat this until everything is merged into one giant cluster.

The Geometry & Metrics

This builds a Dendrogram (a tree diagram). You can slice the tree horizontally at any height to get your desired number of clusters. The core question is: How do we define the distance between two clusters that contain multiple points? This is called the Linkage Criteria:

  • Single Linkage: Distance between the two closest points. (Tends to create long, stringy clusters — called "chaining").
  • Complete Linkage: Distance between the two farthest points. (Creates tight, compact clusters).
  • Ward’s Linkage (Production Standard): Merges clusters that result in the smallest increase in the overall Variance (WCSS).
⚠️ The CDC Complexity Trap

Interviewer: "We have 10 million customer records. Should we use Hierarchical Clustering or K-Means?"

Your Answer: "We must use K-Means (or Mini-Batch K-Means). Hierarchical clustering has a time complexity of \(\mathcal{O}(N^3)\) and requires an \(\mathcal{O}(N^2)\) distance matrix to be held in RAM. For 10 million rows, our servers will instantly run out of memory. Hierarchical is strictly for small datasets."

Chapter 53

DBSCAN (Density-Based Spatial Clustering)

The Intuition & Applicability: This solves the crescent-moon problem of K-Means. DBSCAN doesn't care about means or spheres; it looks for densely packed "islands" of data separated by empty "oceans." Imagine mapping a viral disease outbreak using GPS coordinates. Infections follow roads, rivers, and dense neighborhoods, not perfect circles. DBSCAN organically maps this non-linear shape, while isolating random, unrelated cases in rural areas as noise.

The Parameters

You do not specify \(K\). You specify two physical rules:

  1. \(\epsilon\) (Epsilon): The radius of a neighborhood around a point.
  2. min_samples: The minimum number of neighbors required inside that \(\epsilon\) radius for a point to be considered a "Core Point".

The Algorithm (The Contagion)

DBSCAN classifies every single point into one of three categories:

  • Core Point: If a point has enough neighbors, it becomes a Core Point.
  • Border Point: Does not have enough neighbors to be a Core Point, but falls physically inside the \(\epsilon\) radius of a Core Point. It is part of the cluster, but cannot spread the infection any further.
  • Noise (Outlier): Any point that is not a Core Point and is not reachable by a Core Point.

The cluster grows organically like a puddle of water or a contagion spread.

Chapter 54

Dimensionality Reduction & PCA

The Intuition: You have a 1,000-dimensional dataset, but much of it is noise or highly correlated. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) finds a new, smaller set of axes (components) that retains the maximum possible variance of the original data. You are mathematically squashing a 3D object into a 2D shadow, but choosing the angle of the light so the shadow keeps the maximum detail.

The Hardcore Math (Linear Algebra Flex)

  1. Center the data matrix \(X\) by subtracting the mean.
  2. Compute the Covariance Matrix: \(C = \frac{1}{n-1} X^T X\).
  3. Find the Eigenvectors and Eigenvalues of \(C\).
    • The Eigenvectors are the new physical axes (Principal Components).
    • The Eigenvalues dictate exactly how much variance is captured by that specific axis.
Chapter 54b (Deep Dive)

The Mathematics of SVD vs. Covariance

⚠️ The Day 1 System Design Trap

Interviewer: "Computing \(X^T X\) for a massive text dataset is taking forever and losing precision. How do we fix PCA?"

Your Answer: "We never explicitly calculate the covariance matrix in production. We perform Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) directly on the centered data matrix \(X\). SVD decomposes \(X = U \Sigma V^T\). The columns of \(V\) give us our Principal Components, and the singular values in \(\Sigma\) squared give us our Eigenvalues. It is vastly more numerically stable and computationally efficient."

1. The Intuition: The Floating-Point Cliff

If a feature has a tiny value like \(10^{-5}\), explicitly calculating \(X^T X\) forces you to square it to \(10^{-10}\). Computers run out of standard floating-point precision, resulting in underflow (rounding to 0.0), destroying delicate variance. Large numbers cause overflow. Squaring the matrix squares its Condition Number.

2. The Whiteboard Proof: Why SVD = PCA

Step 1: The SVD Definition

Any matrix \(X\) can be decomposed: \(X = U \Sigma V^T\).

Step 2 & 3: The Algebraic Unfolding

Plug into the covariance formula: \(X^T X = (U \Sigma V^T)^T (U \Sigma V^T)\)

Using transpose rules: \(= (V \Sigma^T U^T)(U \Sigma V^T)\)

Because \(U\) is orthogonal, \(U^T U = I\). Thus: \(X^T X = V \Sigma^2 V^T\)

Step 4: The Checkmate

This final equation is the exact definition of Eigendecomposition (\(A = Q \Lambda Q^{-1}\)). By performing SVD directly on \(X\), the matrix \(V\) gives us the exact Eigenvectors, and \(\Sigma^2\) gives the Eigenvalues. We never calculated \(X^T X\).

The Production Workflow: How NumPy / LAPACK Does It

Production libraries use a two-step iterative numerical method instead of squaring:

  1. Householder Reflections (The Broadsword): Uses geometric "mirrors" to reflect points and force numbers outside the main/super diagonal to exactly zero (Bidiagonalization).
  2. QR Algorithm (The Scalpel): Applies tiny iterative Givens rotations to shrink the super-diagonal elements to zero, seamlessly converging on the Singular Values. Modern methods use "Divide and Conquer" to parallelize this on CPU cores.
Chapter 56

Regression Metrics (The Cost of Being Wrong)

MetricMathIntuition & Traps
MAE (Mean Absolute Error) \(\frac{1}{m} \sum |y_i - \hat{y}_i|\) Average physical distance. L1 Norm. Penalizes linearly. Trap: Not differentiable at 0.
MSE (Mean Squared Error) \(\frac{1}{m} \sum (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2\) L2 Norm. Magnifies outliers (error of 10 → 100 penalty). Trap: Units are squared, confusing to business stakeholders.
RMSE \(\sqrt{\text{MSE}}\) Brings penalty back to original units. Heavily punishes extreme deviations compared to MAE.
R² (Coefficient of Determination) \(1 - \frac{\text{SSR}}{\text{SST}}\) How much better your model is than statically guessing the mean. Trap: Can be negative if your model is worse than the mean.
Chapters 57-60

Classification: The Imbalanced Data Trap & Confusion Matrix

The Flaw of Accuracy: You build a Fraud detector (99.9% Safe, 0.1% Fraud). A broken model that hardcodes return "Safe" achieves 99.9% accuracy but catches zero fraud. Accuracy is a useless, dangerous metric for imbalanced data.

The Confusion Matrix (The Ultimate Truth Table)

\[ \begin{array}{c|c|c} & \text{Predicted: Negative} & \text{Predicted: Positive} \\ \hline \text{Actual: Negative} & \mathbf{True \ Negatives \ (TN)} & \mathbf{False \ Positives \ (FP)} \text{ (Type I)} \\ \hline \text{Actual: Positive} & \mathbf{False \ Negatives \ (FN)} \text{ (Type II)} & \mathbf{True \ Positives \ (TP)} \end{array} \]

Precision, Recall & F1

  • Precision = \(\frac{TP}{TP + FP}\): "Out of everything my model claimed was positive, how many actually were?" Punishes False Alarms.
  • Recall = \(\frac{TP}{TP + FN}\): "Out of all actual positives hiding in the real world, how many did my model find?" Punishes Misses.
  • F1-Score = \(2 \times \frac{P \times R}{P + R}\): Harmonic mean. Mathematically collapses toward the lowest value, heavily punishing models that completely sacrifice one metric.

⭐ The Probabilistic Definition (Memorize This)

AUC represents the exact probability that if I pick one random positive instance and one random negative instance from my dataset, my model will correctly assign a higher probability score to the positive instance than the negative one.
